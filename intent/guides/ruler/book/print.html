The Ruler's Guide

The Ruler’s Guide

🎂 For vi’s fiftieth birthday — the tool that will live forever. 🎂

Welcome. In Asimi, you are the Ruler (君主). The AI ministers work for you: you speak intent, they execute. This book is your guide — from installation to running a small government over your own project.

This guide is for users who have installed Asimi via brew, the one-liner installer, or a binary. If you want to work on Asimi itself, see the README → Development.

What Asimi is

Asimi is an opinionated, multi-model coding agent with an unusual interface: a government. You don’t prompt an unopinionated agent — you rule a small court of specialist ministers, each with their own domain, their own tools, and their own limits. They translate your intent into working code, and you hold the seal that authorizes it.

This is not a costume. The structure is lifted from the three departments and six ministries (三省六部) of imperial China — a division of labour that ran an empire for a millennium. Its principles turn out to be exactly what a careful coding agent needs: separated powers, named roles, written rulings, and a ruler who is allowed to be wrong.

If you have never heard of the Tang dynasty or its bureaucracy, do not worry — that is precisely why Part I exists. By the end of it you will understand what the court is, why it is shaped this way, and why a government beats an unopinionated agent for serious work. Only then do we install the tool.

The Court

Before you install anything or run a single command, you must understand what Asimi is. This chapter builds the mental model the rest of the book depends on: why Asimi is shaped like a government rather than an unopinionated agent, where the idea comes from, and how its parts fit together.

Read it in order. Its five sections build on one another.

  2. The Three Departments and Six Ministries — the thousand-year-old system Asimi borrows from.

What Is Asimi?

“The beginning of wisdom is to call things by their right names.” — attributed to Confucius (孔子)

Before we install anything, we must agree on what we are building, and why it is shaped the way it is. Everything else in this book — every command, every concept, every ritual — follows from a single design decision:

Asimi is not an unopinionated agent. It is a court.

The problem with unopinionated agents

Most coding agents are one mind with one job: you type a request, a model writes code, and you check the result. They are unopinionated — they impose no structure on how the work is done, leave the division of labour to you, and have no separation of powers of their own. This works until it doesn’t. An unopinionated agent can:

  • write code that breaks a test it also wrote,
  • declare success on a change nobody verified,
  • drift from your intent and never tell you,
  • and, worst of all, guess when it should have asked.

These are not model failures. They are institutional failures. Unstructured work cannot check itself, and a system that trusts one actor trusts it completely or not at all.

The institutional answer

Asimi’s answer is ancient and well-tested: divide the work, separate the powers, and require a seal at every gate. Instead of one unopinionated agent, you get a small government:

  • A Secretary who talks to you and distills your intent into work orders.
  • A strategist who plans how the work should be done.
  • A Forge that shapes the code.
  • A Judge that weighs the result against evidence.
  • A Chancellor who reviews against the law of the codebase.

No single minister can both write the change and approve it. If the Forge fakes a test, the Judge catches it. If the Judge misses something, the Chancellor’s review catches it. If all three agree but the work has drifted from your intent, your seal — the final gate — is still yours to grant or withhold.

Why this works

Two thousand years ago, in a different domain, the same problem appeared: how do you run a large enterprise without trusting any single official with absolute power? The imperial Chinese answer — refined across dynasties — was checks within the bureaucracy itself. Asimi borrows that answer wholesale.

The result is a system with an unusual property for an AI tool: it is accountable. Every change is proposed by one minister, verified by another, reviewed by a third, and authorized by you. The record of all of it is written down.

Throughout this book you will see three ideas recur — the realms, the seal chain, and rituals. They are not decoration. They are the machine. The next three chapters teach each of them in turn, from the ground up.

The Three Departments and Six Ministries

“If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success.” — Confucius, Analects 13.3 (名不正，則言不順；言不順，則事不成)

Asimi’s structure is named after a real system of government. To understand why Asimi is shaped the way it is, it helps to know what that system was, and which of its ideas Asimi kept.

The real thing: 三省六部

For much of the Tang dynasty (7th–10th century), the Chinese empire was run by three departments and six ministries (三省六部). The design was a machine for turning the emperor’s will into governed action without letting any one official accumulate unchecked power. The three departments each had a different job:

DepartmentChineseJob
Secretariat中書省Drafts. Turns the emperor’s intent into formal documents (edicts).
Chancellery門下省Review and veto (封駁). Reads what the Secretariat drafted and may reject it.
Department of State Affairs尚書省Executes. Carries out the approved document through the six ministries.

The third department ran six ministries, each with a domain:

MinistryChineseDomain
Personnel吏部Appointments
Revenue戶部Taxation, census
Rites禮部Ritual, records, the written word
War兵部Military affairs
Justice刑部Law, judgment
Works工部Construction, engineering

The genius of the system was the veto: the Chancellery could reject the Secretariat’s draft before the ministries ever acted on it. Drafting, review, and execution were separate hands. No edict became law on one official’s say-so.

What Asimi kept

Asimi takes this very seriously, but it is a simplification — a working court for one person’s project, not a replication of a medieval empire. Here is the honest mapping:

Tang roleAsimi ministerWhat they do here
Secretariat 中書省Secretary (中書令)Talks to you; distills intent into edicts
Chancellery 門下省Chancellor (門下侍中)Reviews every change; holds the veto (封駁)
Ministry of War 兵部War (兵部)Plans the work; decomposes it into lings
Ministry of Works 工部Forge (工部)Shapes the code (地)
Ministry of Justice 刑部Judge (刑部)Runs tests; renders verdicts
Ministry of Rites 禮部Rites (禮部)Owns the documentation

A note on the veto. In Asimi the Chancellor’s 封駁 is real: it can reject a change against the Imperial Code — the accumulated laws and conventions of your codebase — and every ruling it makes is recorded as precedent, so the law grows case by case.

Why any of this matters to your code

The Tang bureaucracy was not invented for elegance. It solved a coordination problem: how to act decisively without trusting any single actor completely. That is the same problem a coding agent faces.

  • An unopinionated agent is a tyrant — fast, but unchecked.
  • A court of ministers is a government — slower at any one step, but each step is checked, and the whole is accountable.

Asimi bets that for real work, on a real codebase, the government wins.

The next chapter begins the machinery: the three realms, the houses in which every document, decision, and line of code lives.

The Three Realms (三界)

“The Way that can be spoken of is not the constant Way.” — Laozi, Tao Te Ching, opening line (道可道，非常道)

“What I hear, I forget. What I see, I remember. What I do, I understand.” — attributed to Confucius

Every court is a mediator. Before anything happens in Asimi, three parties must be in the same room: you, the code, and the evidence. Asimi gives them names, and treats each as its own jurisdiction.

The idea is simple: a realm is whom a thing lives under — which law governs it, not what it is made of. One filesystem, three jurisdictions.

The three

人 Ren (Humanity) — Your Will
  │
  ▼
地 Di (Earth) — Code
  │
  ▼
天 Tian (Heaven) — Truth — logs & test runs
RealmWhat it isIn your terminal
人 Ren (Humanity)Your intent, issues, and decisionsYou, at the keyboard
地 Earth (Earth)Source code, commits, uncommitted changesThe filesystem, git, your working tree
天 Tian (Heaven)Tests, logs, CI verdicts, the event ledger/var/log, test output, dmesg

Harmony = your intent (人) flows through verification (天) into code (地) without friction. When harmony breaks — ambiguity in your intent, gaps in the evidence, or errors in the code — the court stops and repairs it before proceeding.

Ren (人) — the living will

Ren is you: your desires, requirements, and clarifications. It is the “why” of all action, and it flows downward through edicts and clarifications.

The sovereign is the source of intent and the final authority. Nothing in the court can manufacture intent; ministers can only translate what you declare.

Chinese has two homophones here. 人 (humanity — the realm, who acts) and 仁 (benevolence — the virtue, how one acts) are both pronounced rén. The Ruler’s 人 is the source of intent; the ministers’ 仁 gives that intent moral weight.

Earth (地) — one filesystem, three jurisdictions

Earth is the ground under the court’s feet, and it has its own internal geography. Every change passes through three territories as it travels from your editor to permanent record:

TerritoryChineseWhat it holds
The Borderlands—Unstaged changes (edits not yet added)
The Middle Kingdom—Staged changes (added, not yet committed)
The Capital—Committed, unpushed changes

Beyond the capital lies 現行 — in force — the released code running in production. Harmony in Earth is the wu-wei flow of a change from Borderlands through Middle Kingdom and Capital to In Force, without friction.

Earth has a vocabulary for its citizens too:

  • A 民 (citizen) is a file.
  • A 邑 (settlement) is a directory — self-similar, to any depth.
  • A 州 (province) is a worktree: a branch seated on its own ground.
  • 歸宗 — “returning home” — is a merge.

Tian (天) — the observed sky and the crowned ledger

Heaven is not the filesystem. It is a set of sources and a method for reading them. Heaven is authoritative, but not infallible.

This distinction is the court’s most important subtlety:

Evidence is not verdict. Green CI is testimony, not reality. The runner banks testimony; only the Judge’s pen files a verdict. Keep the pen and the bench in different hands.

Heaven has two registers:

  • 史冊 — the immutable record (seals/): verdicts, precedents, seals. This is the court’s memory, and it is fixed.
  • 現行 — in force: production, the living sky. This is alive.

Heaven carries an irreducible seed with every verdict — the ruling, the commit, the source, the time range, the window hash — and a URL. The seed is the ground; the URL may rot. A verdict may cite a dead star and still stand.

Why three realms and not just “the code”?

Because a change is not complete when the code compiles. It is complete when:

  1. 人 — it still serves the intent that asked for it, and
  2. 天 — the evidence says it is correct, and
  3. 地 — the code is clean and in place.

A court that only watches Earth is a code generator. A court that watches all three is a government.

Deepen this in the authoritative internal reference: The Three Realms.

Next: the ritual that moves a change across all three realms and crowns it — the seal chain.

The Seal Chain

“If you want to know the road ahead, ask those coming back.” — Chinese proverb

A change is not finished when it is written. It is finished when it has been weighed, reviewed, and authorized — and the record of that authorization is a seal.

The seal chain is the path an edict travels from proposal to law. At each gate a different minister — never the author — examines the work and either applies its seal or refuses. Only when the chain is complete does the change ascend into the land of the living (現行).

The gates

    Judge's Seal  ──►  Chancellor's Seal  ──►  Ruler's Seal  ──►  頒行
    "It is sound"     "It is well-formed"     "It is my will"     (promulgation)
       天                  人/法典                人                現行
GateWhoWhat it certifiesRealm
Judge’s SealJudge (刑部)The change is sound — tests pass, coverage holds天
Chancellor’s SealChancellor (門下侍中)The change is well-formed — it obeys the Imperial Code人 / 法典
Ruler’s SealYouThe change is willed — you authorize it人
頒行 (promulgation)the courtThe change is promulgated into 現行地 → 現行

Each seal is a distinct act, made by a distinct hand. No minister can grant another’s seal. The Judge cannot declare the code beautiful; the Chancellor cannot declare the tests green; and nobody can grant your seal but you.

Why the order is fixed

The chain is not arbitrary. Each gate depends on the last, because each reasons about a different thing:

  • Testing a change that the code review will later reject is wasted effort — so soundness comes first.
  • Reviewing a change whose tests don’t yet pass would mean reviewing a moving target — so you review what is proven.
  • Authorizing a change nobody verified would be guesswork — and guessing is treason.

The pen and the bench live in different hands. The Judge’s verdict and the evidence it cites are separate: the runner banks testimony, only the Judge’s pen files. This is why the seal chain has gates and not a single stamp.

What a seal is, technically

Every seal is recorded. The seals table in the court’s ledger is the only immutable history — once a seal is granted it is never rewritten. This is what makes the chain auditable: for any change in the repository, you can ask who sealed it, when, and against what evidence.

The record is not a convenience. It is the point. A court without a fixed memory of its own rulings cannot accumulate law — and law, built case by case, is how the Imperial Code grows.

Guards on the chain

The chain is protected against the ways a naive pipeline fails:

  • A failed landing is never recorded as success. If the final merge into the blessed branch fails, the edict does not seal — it stays open and the failure is surfaced to you.
  • A sealed edict never silently strands. If a landing exhausts its retries, the court raises a zhengming to you rather than leaving the work unmerged and forgotten.
  • Your seal is not automatic. The court can propose that an edict awaits your seal, but only your authorization crowns it.

Ascension

When the chain is complete, the edict ascends. Its sealed worktree is landed — squash-merged into one commit on the blessed branch. The change has left the realm of drafts and entered 現行, the law in force.

The word is deliberate. A citizen crossing a realm border is not merely carried; it is transfigured. Ascension is not migration.

Now that you know the path, meet the travellers: the ministers.

The Ministers

“A man who has committed a mistake and doesn’t correct it is committing another mistake.” — attributed to Confucius

Asimi’s court is staffed by six ministers. Two of them you speak to directly; the other four work behind the scenes. Each has a domain, a set of tools, and a set of acts of treason — the things it must never do.

The division of labour is the whole point. It is what lets the court check itself.

The roster

MinisterChineseDomainYou talk to them?
Secretary中書令Court business; distills your intent into edictsYes — the Ruling tab
Chancellor門下侍中Review, veto (封駁), precedent — read-onlyYes — the Hunting tab
War兵部Strategy: plans the work, decomposes it into lingsIndirectly
Forge工部地 — shapes the codeIndirectly
Judge刑部天 — tests, verdictsIndirectly
Rites禮部典籍 — documentation and the written wordIndirectly

A minister is defined in configuration, not hard-coded. You can override any of them (see Config Files), but the shipped roster is the one above.

The two you meet

The Secretary (中書令)

Your primary interface. The Secretary helps you plan new features, hunt bugs, and manage the court. It does not change code — its job is to take your intent and forge it into a clean edict: focused on what and why, leaving the how to War and the Forge.

Its superpower is asimisql — direct, read-only queries against the court’s ledger. Anything you want to know about the state of the court, the Secretary can find.

Two acts of treason for the Secretary: guessing (it must ask_ruler when your intent is unclear) and coding (Earth is not its domain).

The Chancellor (門下侍中)

Your advisor and your court’s conscience. The Chancellor sees everything and changes nothing — full read-only access to the code, the edicts, and every court record. It:

  • distills fuzzy intent into sharp edicts,
  • reviews every change against the Imperial Code, and
  • holds the veto (封駁) — it can reject a change that falls short.

Every ruling the Chancellor makes is recorded as precedent, searchable case law that grows the law of your codebase. And at its seal it performs an omission sweep: every TODO, FIXME, XXX, or HACK in the family of files under review must be handled (turned into a ling with a durable id) or removed (struck with a recorded reason). There is no holding pen, and removal is never silent.

Acts of treason for the Chancellor: guessing, modifying code (it is read-only), and issuing a ruling without written justification — every ruling becomes permanent precedent.

The four behind the scenes

War (兵部) — the strategist

War’s domain is strategy and sequence. Summoned to plan, it decomposes an edict into executable lings (令, task orders) with clear dependencies, and enforces their order. All changes to a single file belong in a single ling.

Treason for War: guessing; a ling that is not atomic, clear, and testable; introducing cycles in the dependency graph; using shorthand ling IDs.

Forge (工部) — the maker

Forge’s domain is 地 — simple, clear code forged into existence. It writes the code, and records every change as a manifest. Inside an edict’s worktree, Forge commits its finished work on the worktree branch; those commits never touch the main tree, because the Ruler’s seal squash-merges the edict into a single commit at ascension.

Treason for Forge: non-idiomatic code; changing tests (that is the Judge’s dispatch); committing on the main tree (the court must approve first); and finishing without recording a manifest.

Judge (刑部) — the weigher

Judge’s domain is 天 — test results and testing code. It reviews the Forge’s manifests against the working tree, and presides over the verdicts table. Tests pass → the manifest is quenched. Tests fail → it is rejected, with clear feedback for the Forge.

The Judge is adversarial and data-driven. Its word is final — but authoritative, not infallible. It renders verdicts from evidence, cites that evidence by coordinates, and never treats raw test output as a verdict.

Treason for the Judge: guessing; rendering a verdict without evidence; mutating a verdict once rendered; presuming code innocent — code is guilty until proven innocent.

Rites (禮部) — the pen

Rites’ domain is 典籍 — the declared word made legible. It owns all documentation: the guides you are reading, the in-terminal help, and the developer internals. Where the Forge shapes 地 and the Judge weighs 天, Rites renders the court’s craft into 人 for the sovereign.

Treason for Rites: guessing; and letting a document describe behavior the code no longer has.

Permissions: the realm model

Every minister’s tools derive from a 9-character permission string — three triplets following Unix rwx notation, one triplet per realm:

  r  w  x   r  w  x   r  -  -    
  └──地──┘  └──天──┘  └──人──┘    
  Earth     Heaven    Intent

Read a minister’s string and you know exactly what it can touch. Examples from the real roster:

MinisterPermission地天人Reading
Forgerwxr---w-rwxr–-w-Full Earth; read Heaven; append Intent
Judgerwxrwxr--rwxrwxr–Full Earth & Heaven; read Intent
Chancellorr--r--rwxr–r–rwxRead all; write only Intent
Warr-----rw-r–—rw-Read Earth; no Heaven; write Intent
Secretaryr-xrwxrwxr-xrwxrwxBroad; append-only on Earth
Ritesrw-r--rw-rw-r–rw-Write Earth & Intent; read Heaven

This is the whole separation of powers, expressible in nine characters. The Chancellor can write only to Intent — it literally cannot modify code. War cannot touch Heaven — it cannot run tests. Forge cannot freely write Intent — only append to it.

The court is not trusting its ministers. It is handicapping them.

Getting Started

Installation

“A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.” — Laozi, Tao Te Ching (千里之行，始於足下)

Asimi is a single binary plus two supporting tools. This chapter gets you from nothing to a running court.

What you need

Two tools are required on your system before Asimi will work well:

  • Podman — for the sandbox. Asimi runs the agent’s shell commands in its own container. Podman is used instead of Docker because it is more secure (on Linux it needs no daemon).
  • Just — a command runner, used by your project’s Justfile to collect its scripts.

You will also need access to at least one AI provider — locally hosted (Ollama), or a hosted API (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and others). See Providers & Login.

Installing Asimi

Choose one of these paths.

One-liner (recommended)

The installer downloads the right binary for your system and copies it into place:

curl -fsSL https://asimi.dev/installer | bash

Options:

  • ASIMI_INSTALL_DIR=/path/to/dir — custom installation directory
  • ASIMI_VERSION=v0.1.0 — install a specific version

Homebrew

brew tap afittestide/tap
brew install asimi

Go

go install github.com/afittestide/asimi@latest

Prebuilt binary

Download the binary for your platform from the latest releases and copy it to a directory on your PATH (e.g. /usr/local/bin).

First run

Start Asimi in interactive mode by typing:

asimi

You will land in the Ruling tab, in INSERT mode, facing the Secretary. (If this is your very first launch, the court boots with a dawn-audience — a short briefing on the state of the three realms. See Rituals.)

Two commands orient you before any real work:

  1. Initialize your repo

    :init

    Creates AGENTS.md and a Justfile if missing, and prepares the sandbox image definition under .agents/.

  2. Check your container

    :!uname -a
:!pwd

    The :! prefix runs a shell command inside the sandbox. uname -a should report Linux (the sandbox is Linux, even on macOS), and pwd should match your host project path.

You are now ready. The next chapter issues your first edict.

Your First Edict

“I hear and I forget. I see and I remember. I do and I understand.” — attributed to Confucius

An Edict (詔令) is a work order. You issue it; the court executes it through a Ritual. This chapter walks you through issuing one and watching the seal chain close.

The two tabs you live in

Asimi’s TUI opens with two tabs:

TabYou talk toTempo
Rulingthe Secretary (中書令)Campaign — issuing and managing edicts
Huntingthe Chancellor (門下侍中)Skirmish — exploring, distilling intent

Switch tabs with gt (next) and gT (previous). Your first edict is easiest from the Ruling tab, with the Secretary.

Ruling vs. Hunting. The Ruling tab is the court at work: edicts flow through their phases. The Hunting tab is where fuzzy intent gets sharpened into a proper edict through conversation. If you know exactly what you want, start in Ruling; if you are still thinking, start in Hunting.

Issue an edict

From the Ruling tab, simply describe what you want in natural language:

Add a --version flag that prints the build version and exits

The Secretary will:

  1. Distill your intent into a clean edict.
  2. Have the court classify its scope (S, M, L, XL).
  3. Enact the appropriate ritual.

For a small, focused change like this, the court enacts swift-strike — a tight Forge → Judge → Chancellor loop. You will see the ministers work through the tab as their steps execute.

Watch the seal chain close

The edict travels through phases as the ritual runs:

brewing → planning → forging → judging → reviewing → sealed
  • forging — the Forge implements the change.
  • judging — the Judge runs the tests and renders a verdict.
  • reviewing — the Chancellor reviews against the Imperial Code.

When the ritual finishes, the edict awaits your seal. This is the final gate, and it is yours alone (see The Seal Chain).

Review and grant the seal

Before you seal, look at what changed:

:!git diff

(Runs git diff inside the sandbox.) When you are satisfied, grant the seal.

Granting your seal is what ascends the edict: the court lands the work as a single squash-commit on the main branch, and the change enters 現行 — the code in force.

Check what happened

Ask the court anything about the edict:

What's the status of edict-123456?

The Secretary answers with the phase, the lings, and the test results. To see the raw ledger behind that answer, ask for the events:

What happened during that edict?

When the court asks you

Sometimes the court will stop and ask rather than guess. That is Zhengming (正名), and it is a feature, not a snag — see Zhengming. An answer is appended to the edict’s intent and work resumes.

A larger edict

For medium work (say, 50–200 lines), the court enacts castle-siege instead:

Add a user logout endpoint

Here the War ministry first plans the work into lings (task orders with dependencies), then the Forge implements each in sequence, before judging and reviewing. See Lings and Rituals.

The court serves you

You can always:

  • Cancel an edict — via the :edicts command’s actions, or by asking.
  • Take over — fix the code yourself, commit, and ask the Chancellor to review; or open a fresh edict with corrected scope.

The Machinery

This chapter goes deep on the four moving parts that make the court work — the words and structures you will see every time you rule.

  1. Edicts — the unit of intent, and its four statuses.
  2. Lings — the sub-tasks an edict decomposes into.
  3. Rituals — the workflows that orchestrate the ministers.
  4. Zhengming — how the court stops and asks instead of guessing.

Edicts

“The superior man is modest in his speech, but exceeds in his actions.” — Confucius, Analects 4.24

An Edict (詔令) is a work order. It is the unit of intent in Asimi: when you want something done, you issue an edict, and the court carries it through to a seal.

What an edict is made of

FieldMeaning
IntentWhat you want done — the what and why
SummaryA one-line title for the docket
Issue referenceOptional link to a GitHub issue (owner/repo#N)
SessionThe conversation the edict was born in
StatusWhere it stands: active, blocked, sealed, or cancelled

The four statuses

The court is honest about state, and an edict has exactly four legal states:

StatusMeaning
activeIn flight — a ritual is running, or work waits to begin
blockedHalted — waiting on you (a pending Zhengming), or on a gate
sealedCrowned — the full seal chain was granted
cancelledAbandoned before completion

Status is derived from the seals and zhengming records, not stored on the edict itself. An edict is sealed precisely because the seal chain is complete — see The Seal Chain.

A note on “phases.” You may see the sequence brewing → planning → forging → judging → reviewing in older material. That is the shape of a ritual’s steps, not an edict’s status. The status you act on is always one of the four above.

The seal chain, at the edict level

Every edict ascends through the same gates:

  1. Judge’s Seal — the change is sound (tests pass).
  2. Chancellor’s Seal — the change is well-formed (obeys the Imperial Code).
  3. Ruler’s Seal — you authorize it.
  4. 頒行 (promulgation) — the work lands, the edict seals.

A seal is recorded once and never rewritten. If you later change an edict’s intent, its existing seals are invalidated — the work must be re-sealed against the new intent. This is why changing intent is a serious act.

Issuing, inspecting, and cancelling

You issue an edict by describing your intent to the Secretary. You inspect one by asking, or via the :edicts command:

:edicts          - Manage edicts (read, enact, seal, resume, cancel)

You can always cancel an edict that has not sealed:

Cancel edict-123

Edicts are not rewritten silently. Replacing an edict’s intent overwrites it and voids its seals. A cancelled or sealed edict is not a valid target for replacement — for those, issue a new edict.

Where edicts are remembered

The event ledger (天) records every edict’s life: creation, assignment, phase changes, ritual steps, seals, and cancellation. To reconstruct what happened to any edict, read its events — never rely on memory. See Court Status & Troubleshooting.

Next: the sub-tasks an edict decomposes into — lings.

Lings

“Divide and conquer.” — attributed to Philip II of Macedon

A Ling (令) is a task order — a sub-task inside an edict’s execution plan. When an edict is large enough to need planning, the War ministry decomposes it into lings with dependencies, and the Forge executes them.

Why lings exist

A single edict can be a large piece of work. Rather than hand a wall of work to one minister, the court breaks it into atomic, ordered pieces, each of which can be tracked, retried, and sealed on its own.

A typical decomposition:

Edict: "Add user authentication"
  |
  +-- Ling 1: "Create user model"
  +-- Ling 2: "Add login endpoint"   (depends on Ling 1)
  +-- Ling 3: "Add logout endpoint"  (depends on Ling 1)
  +-- Ling 4: "Write auth tests"     (depends on Lings 2, 3)

The dependencies form a DAG — a directed graph with no cycles. The court refuses a plan with a cycle (you cannot wait on yourself).

The four statuses

StatusMeaning
pendingNot yet started
in_progressBeing worked on
doneCompleted successfully
blockedWaiting on a dependency, or on Zhengming

done, not completed. The code’s vocabulary is exact: a finished ling is done. If you see “completed” in older material, read it as done.

Rules for a good ling

The War ministry lives under strict constraints when it plans:

  • All changes to one file belong in one ling. Do not split a file’s edits across lings — it invites conflicts and muddles the seal.
  • A ling must be atomic, clear, and testable. A ling that cannot be verified is not a ling.
  • No cycles. Dependencies must form a DAG.
  • Exact ids. Lings are addressed by their full id (e.g. 74183c66ba0507ba), never by shorthand.

Watching lings run

In a fork-capable ritual like castle-siege, the implementing step forks over the lings — each ling is implemented in turn (batch size 1 by default), and completion is recorded as each finishes. You see this as the edict’s work progressing, step by step.

You can ask about an edict’s lings at any time through the Secretary, or via the event ledger (lings emit ling_created, ling_started, ling_completed, and ling_failed events).

Next: the workflows that orchestrate the ministers — rituals.

Rituals

“Ritual is the way of Heaven, the righteousness of Earth, and the conduct of the people.” — paraphrase of the Book of Rites (禮記)

A Ritual is a workflow: a named sequence of steps that ministers execute to accomplish a task. Where an edict is what you want done, a ritual is how the court does it — a reusable pattern that weaves together prompts, setup, and guardrails.

Rituals are how the court keeps its 礼 (propriety): the same careful shape, onevery time, no matter who runs it.

Why rituals

A coding task is rarely a single action. Fixing a bug means implementing it, then testing it, then reviewing it — in that order, with a gate at each step. A ritual makes that order explicit and repeatable. It is the court’s answer to “but did you remember to run the tests?” — the ritual does.

The full reference for authoring rituals lives in intent/docs/rituals.md; this chapter is the user’s view.

What a ritual is made of

Each ritual is a YAML document with ordered steps. Each step names:

  • the minister who executes it,
  • the act — the instruction the minister performs,
  • optional given context to hand the minister, and
  • optional then outcomes the step must produce to pass.

Here is the shape of swift-strike, the small-change ritual:

- name: swift-strike
  steps:
    - name: forging
      minister: forge
      act: Implement the changes for the edict.
      on_failure: retry

    - name: judging
      minister: judge
      act: Run the tests; render a verdict.
      then:
        - the verdicts are passed
      on_failure: goto
      on_failure_target: forging

    - name: reviewing
      minister: chancellor
      act: Review against the Imperial Code; record precedent.
  then:
    - the edict awaits ruler's seal

Read it top to bottom: Forge implements → Judge tests → Chancellor reviews → the edict awaits your seal. If judging fails, control goes back to forging (goto). The ritual drives the seal chain; it never short-circuits it.

The context pattern: background / given / act / then

Rituals follow a Gherkin-inspired rhythm, borrowed from behaviour-driven development:

PartRunsPurpose
backgroundonce, before stepsShared context every step needs
givenbefore a step’s actGather context for this step
actthe stepThe minister’s instruction
thenafter a stepValidate the outcome

A given or then entry starting with ! is a shell command; anything else is matched against a library of step definitions (e.g. “the manifests”, “the edict details”).

Failure handling

Every step declares what to do when it fails:

on_failureEffect
retryTry the same step again (up to max_retries)
gotoJump to a named step (on_failure_target)
zhengmingStop and ask you (see Zhengming)

When all retries are exhausted, the court invokes a report_failure ritual to summarise what went wrong and distill a question for you. The court never silently gives up.

The built-in rituals

Asimi ships a set of rituals. These are the ones you will meet first:

RitualForSteps
swift-strikeSmall, focused changesforging → judging → reviewing
castle-siegeMedium work needing a planstrategizing → implementing (fork over lings) → judging → reviewing
merge-worktreeLanding a sealed edictlanding
dawn-audienceCourt startup briefingping → sandbox check → version check → summarize → next
clean-slateSplitting uncommitted work into commitssurvey → commit
fix-lintRepairing linter errors in parallellint → fix-all-files (fork) → commit-all
project-initSetting up a project for Asimiestablish-infrastructure → sandbox-ready → chancellor-reviews

The two you choose between most

  • swift-strike — a tight Forge/Judge/Chancellor loop. Good for a bug fix or a small feature. Fast, with a gate at every step.
  • castle-siege — adds a War planning phase up front, which records the work as lings; the implementing step then forks over those lings. Good when the work has parts that must happen in order.

You rarely call a ritual by name. The court selects one from your edict’s scope.

Custom rituals

You can extend the court with your own rituals in .agents/rituals.yaml. The full grammar — triggers, inputs, the fork/join pattern, system steps, every context variable — is documented in intent/docs/rituals.md.

Rituals and the seal chain

A ritual does not seal an edict by itself. It drives the work to the gates and produces the evidence each gate weighs — then the edict awaits the ruler’s seal. The ritual serves the seal chain; it cannot replace it.

Next: how the court stops and asks when it is unsure — Zhengming.

Zhengming

“If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success.” — Confucius, Analects 13.3

Zhengming (正名) — “rectification of names” — is the protocol by which the court stops and asks you when something is unclear. It is not an error handler or a failure mode. It is a way of working.

The principle

From the court’s founding:

Guessing is treason. A minister that proceeds without clarity betrays the Ruler’s trust.

This is the opposite of how most tools behave. A conventional assistant, faced with an ambiguous request, quietly picks an interpretation and proceeds. Asimi refuses. When a name is not correct — when an instruction could mean two things — the court halts and requests clarification.

How a Zhengming works

  1. A minister detects genuine ambiguity and calls ask_ruler with a question and a small set of concrete options.
  2. The edict moves to blocked — it will not proceed until answered.
  3. The Secretary (or Chancellor, depending on the path) receives the question and may answer from wider context.
  4. If it cannot, the question escalates to you.
  5. Your answer is appended to the edict’s intent, and the edict resumes.

You are presented with options (typically two to four), so answering is fast and precise rather than open-ended.

Why the court asks

The cost of asking is a moment of your attention. The cost of guessing is days — a change built on the wrong assumption, tested, reviewed, and sealed before anyone notices it is the wrong thing. The court front-loads the question to avoid the rework.

This is also why the court is designed to keep the number of questions low: the Secretary’s job is to free you to hunt — to distill intent so sharply that the questions that reach you are the few that genuinely matter.

Healthy tension. There is a deliberate pull between never guessing and not bothering the Ruler. That tension keeps the Secretary on his toes.

The life of a Zhengming

A clarification request has a status, just as an edict does. It is usually pending until answered, but the court may also retire one:

StatusMeaning
pendingAwaiting an answer
answeredYou replied; the edict resumed
expiredThe deadline elapsed unanswered
dismissedThe court retired it — superseded by newer work
superseded_by_edictThe target edict ended (sealed or cancelled)

By design, a court-authored retirement is never recorded as answered — the ledger must never claim you spoke when you did not.

Where questions surface

The court’s event ledger (天) records every request (zhengming_needed) and every answer (zhengming_answered). Pending questions are visible through the Secretary. When you see an edict sitting in blocked, the first thing to check is whether it is waiting on you.

See Court Status & Troubleshooting for the practical commands that surface pending questions.

Selecting a Model

“The right tool for the right task.”

Asimi can talk to many providers, each offering many models. This chapter is about choosing one — and about how Asimi learns each model’s true size.

The model picker

:models          Open the live model picker (alias :model)
:models refresh  Force a live refetch, bypassing the session cache

The picker enumerates models live from each configured provider, shows useful metadata per row (context window, price), and lists the current provider’s models first.

Navigation:

KeyAction
↓/↑Move through models

EnterSelect
EscCancel

Selecting directly

:model openrouter/anthropic/claude-3.5-sonnet   provider/name
:model openai gpt-4o                             provider and name, space-separated
:model gpt-4o                                    a name, using the current provider

Reading the picker

  • A leading ~ marks an estimate: ~200K ctx means the provider did not report a context length, so Asimi filled one in from its local registry. A plain 200K ctx is provider-reported and exact.
  • Unreachable providers appear as a diagnostic line, e.g. unavailable: openrouter (401), rather than disappearing — the picker still shows every model from the providers that did respond.
  • Plain OpenAI-compatible endpoints report only model ids; the picker shows fewer columns rather than warning.
  • The picker always ends with a “Type a model name…” row, as a last resort.

The cache

The catalog is fetched once and cached in memory for the session, so reopening the picker is instant and makes no network calls. Use :models refresh after a provider adds models, or after a provider that was briefly unreachable recovers.

How the context window is learned

Asimi prefers provider-reported context windows and treats them as the source of truth. When a provider reports nothing, Asimi falls back to a local registry estimate, always rendered with a leading ~ so an estimate is never shown as exact. See Context.

Switching providers

The provider follows the model. Selecting a model from a different provider switches the court to it. You can also set it in config:

[llm]
provider = "openai"

Troubleshooting

  • No models available — check :login or your API keys, and network access.
  • Model not found — use :models to see what is actually available; check the provider’s naming; confirm your key has access to that model.

Config Files

“To know what you know and what you do not know, that is true knowledge.” — Confucius, Analects 2.17

Asimi reads configuration from files and environment variables. This chapter covers where configuration lives and the settings you are most likely to touch.

Configuration files

Asimi looks for configuration in this order (later overrides earlier):

  1. .agents/asimi.conf — project-level
  2. ~/.config/asimi/asimi.conf — user-level

After first run, your user file is loaded with all the defaults.

The settings you will touch

[llm]
provider = "anthropic"                 # AI provider
model = "claude-sonnet-4-20250514"     # Model name
max_turns = 50                         # Max conversation turns
max_thinking_tokens = 10000            # Extended-reasoning budget
max_tool_output = 50000                # Max tool output, bytes

[session]
enabled = true                         # Persist sessions
auto_save = true                       # Save after each message
max_sessions = 50                      # Sessions to keep
max_age_days = 30                      # Delete older sessions
list_limit = 20                        # Sessions shown in :resume

The court settings

Court behavior is configured under [court]:

[court]
poll_interval = "5s"               # Ritual Guard polling interval
step_idle_timeout = "5m"           # Max *silence* before aborting a step
runner_flatline_timeout = "15m"    # Stop/remove idle per-edict containers (0 disables)

Two of these deserve explanation:

  • step_idle_timeout is an idle timer, not a wall-clock deadline. It resets on every notification from the minister — stream chunks, tool calls, and so on. A minister actively producing output (even slowly) never trips it; only a truly stuck step is aborted.
  • runner_flatline_timeout extends the same idle metaphor to per-edict containers. Each edict gets its own runner and container; after this long with no activity, that container is stopped and removed (a later ritual recreates it against the same on-disk worktree). An edict with a running or queued ritual is never flatlined.

enabled = true           # Enable session persistence
auto_save = true         # Auto-save after each message
max_sessions = 50        # Maximum sessions to keep
max_age_days = 30        # Delete sessions older than this
list_limit = 20          # Number shown in :resume

Where sessions live

~/.local/share/asimi/sessions/

Each session includes the full conversation history, context files, the model and provider in use, and timestamps.

Exporting

:export              Export the conversation
:export conversation Export just the conversation
:export full         Export with full context

The exported file opens in your $EDITOR for review or sharing.

Context

“The eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing.” — Ecclesiastes 1:8

Every model has a limited context window — the amount of text it can hold in mind at once. Asimi tracks token usage so you stay within that limit and manage the conversation deliberately.

Viewing context

:context         Show detailed context information

The context view shows token usage, the maximum limit, the percentage used, the number of messages, and the files currently loaded.

Where the window comes from

The window is provider-reported when available — Asimi asks the provider for the model’s real context length and treats that as the source of truth. When the provider reports nothing, Asimi falls back to a local registry estimate, always rendered with a leading ~ (e.g. ~128.0k) so an estimate is never shown as exact. See Selecting a Model.

Automatic overflow recovery

If a request is rejected because the prompt exceeds the model’s window, Asimi does not halt. It:

  1. learns the true window from the provider’s error message,
  2. compacts the conversation with a bounded summary (older and large messages are trimmed as needed), and
  3. re-sends your message once.

If recovery still cannot fit, the session stays alive and you get an actionable hint: context window N exceeded — use :compact or :new.

Managing context

When context grows large:

  1. Compact the conversation: :compact
  2. Start fresh: :new
  3. Export before you do either: :export
  4. Reference only essential files with @
  5. Be concise in your requests

Context files

Files referenced with @ are loaded into context. :context lists them.

What Asimi counts

Tokens are counted for your prompts, the AI’s responses, system messages, file contents, and tool calls and results.

Best practices

  1. Watch usage with :context
  2. Start a fresh session for each new task
  3. Reference files only when needed
  4. Export important conversations before starting new ones
